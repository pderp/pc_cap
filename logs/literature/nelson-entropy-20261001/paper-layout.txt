Abstract

    A unique, universal definition for the entropy of nonlinear, complex systems is proposed. The Shannon
entropy is shown to measure energetic, configurational, and nonlinear degrees of freedom (DOF). An
entropy calibrated to the asymptotic nonlinearity of the distribution reduces the nonlinear DOF to zero
and modifies the configurational DOF with a generalized logarithm. Physical axioms regarding the
asymptotic growth of states, the calibration from an informational scale, and nonlinear dynamics provide a
foundation for the proof that the calibrated entropy is unique and comprehensive across the short- and
long-range dependency classes. The informational scale quantifies the linear uncertainty independent of the
nonlinearity. This entropy, when constrained by the informational scale, is maximized by the coupled
stretched exponential distributions. A consistent thermodynamics for nonequilibrium systems is proposed
with the temperature equal to the scale constraint divided by the Boltzmann constant. Physical and
informational applications are highlighted.

Introduction
Life has been shown to reside at the edge of chaos [1–3], just one of the motivations for precise models of
complex adaptive systems [4, 5]. Likewise, artificial intelligence systems are pushing the boundaries of safe
control [6–8], necessitating robust designs trained against outliers from fluctuating uncertainty [9]. In these
domains, the difficulty with quantifying heavy-tailed, non-equilibrium [10, 11] phenomena and integrating
these phenomena into foundational methodologies creates an analytical bottleneck restricting analysis and
design.
    The Boltzmann-Gibbs-Shannon (BGS) entropy is well-established as the appropriate uncertainty metric
for equilibrium systems with linear sources of uncertainty [12]. Given moment constraints, the BGS entropy
is maximized by members of the exponential family of distributions, which E.T. Jaynes showed provides
a broad principle for model development [13]. Uncertainty quantification of non-equilibrium systems with
nonlinear sources of uncertainty presents significantly more difficult challenges. First, the moments depend
on both the scale and shape of the distributions, and diverge for large values of the shape parameter.
Secondly, the BGS entropy is quickly dominated by the shape of the distribution, breaking the connection
between the scale and entropy. The generalized Pareto [14, 15] and Gosset (Student’s t) [16] distributions
are shown to be of critical importance for defining a generalized entropy function, since they each utilize a
definition of scale that is independent of the shape of the distribution.
    The scientific investigation of entropy functions for complex systems has, to date, focused on revising the
fourth, additive, Shannon-Khinchin axiom [17]. Given the canonization of the Shannon-Khinchin axioms,
the assumption has been that as long as a more general axiom describing an alternative composition rule
is justified, then the function satisfying this axiom is a generalized entropy function. Examples include the
Havrda-Charvát function [18], popularized by Tsallis [19], and axiomized by Suyari [20], the Sharma-Taneja’s
two-parameter function [21], Tempesta’s group-theory composition [22], and the comprehensive function

proven by Hanel-Thurner using just the first three axioms [23]. However, these mathematical justifications
disconnected entropy from a specific physical measurement.
    Neither Shannon nor Khinchin referred to their observations regarding the properties of the exponential
family and the measurement of information as axioms. Rather, Shannon begins his 1948 treatise on communi-
cation [24] with the pragmatic statement regarding how a measure of information is defined, “The significant
aspect [de-emphasizing semantic meaning] is that the actual message is one selected from a set of possible
messages. ... If the number of messages in a set is finite then this number or a monotonic function [loga-
rithm] of this number can be regarded as a measure of the information produced when one message is chosen
from the set, all choices being equal”. I propose that it is exactly Shannon’s definition that must
be preserved regarding a measure of information in nonlinear, complex systems. To achieve this
goal, I propose a definition of entropy based on the requirement that, given a calibrated growth function for
the equipartition microstates given the degrees of freedom, Wc (N ), the entropy is H(Wc (N )) = N . Three
new axioms are proposed which a) specify the asymptotic scaling properties of microstate growth in com-
plex systems proven by Hanel-Thurner, b) specify a unit of measure based on a unique informational scale,
and c) require that the nonlinear differential equation specifying the probability, pW (N ) is specified by three
constants. From this definition and axioms, I prove that the only function satisfying the requirement to be
a calibrated measurement of the degrees of freedom across the comprehensive classification of the complex
system is the coupled entropy [25,26], now scaled to account for linear extensivity with a stretch nonlinearity.
    There are many nonequilibrium applications where a calibrated measure of entropy will improve analysis.
For example, 5G wireless service is approximately 100 Mbps, where the 100 megabits represent the degrees of
freedom (DOF) for encoding messages. In an interference-free zone, exponential encoding of messages could

enable 21x10 unique messages. Log base 2 entropy returns the bits log2 21x10 = 1x108 . In contrast, a high-
interference environment creates heavy-tailed noise, which would cause the number of messages to grow at
a power-law, rather than exponential rate. The proposed entropy incorporates these nonlinearities into the
metric and is calibrated to still return the number of bits. Calibrated measurement of information is required
across of variety of complex adaptive systems. Another example is the distinction between coding and non-
coding DNA [27,28], in which the four nucleotides form strings of potential messages. Whereas, a direct linear
path between coding DNA and proteins is well-established, the complexity of the non-coding DNA and its
function in regulatory networks is an active area of research. Across the sciences, non-equilibrium systems
such as financial markets [29], socio-political networks [30], ecosystems [31], quantum mechanics [32], energy
fluctuations [33], and many more systems require precise, calibrated nonlinear analysis.
    The specification for the unique, universal entropy function starts with the comprehensive classification
of complex systems proven by Hanel-Thurner [23]. They showed that the asymptotic growth of systems,
and more specifically entropy functions, could be specified by two parameters regarding stretched exponen-
tial growth and power-law (or its inverse super-exponential) growth. I show that this classification can be
made more precise by quantifying the degree of variable nonlinearity (stretch) and the degree of functional
nonlinearity (power-law). From this foundation, I prove that there is a unique unit of measure, which I
refer to as the informational scale. Together, the complexity classification and the unit of measure define
a calibrated entropy and its maximizing distributions, the coupled stretched exponentials. This framework,
known as Nonlinear Statistical Coupling [34], was initiated to improve the methods of Nonextensive Statis-
tical Mechanics (NSM) by focusing on the primary property of complex systems, the nonlinearity. Despite
reports regarding the limitations of NSM [35], corrections to its core problems have not been addressed, so
this paper is more direct in its critique.
    The new contributions reported here include:

   that the entropy function with matching nonlinearities measures the correct degrees of freedom. For the
   equipartition case, the measurement is required to be exclusively the energetic DOF. For the canonical
   ensemble, the measurement is required to be the energetic DOF and a generalized configurational DOF.
   In both cases, the nonlinear DOF are zero.

Definition, uniqueness, and measurement of the informational scale
Specifying the scale of a shape-scale distribution would appear to be an elementary task, but, in fact, the
lack of a clear criterion has resulted in different statistical physics communities using different criteria. For
instance, NSM [39] uses a scale derived from the function Tsallis hypothesized for entropy, while the space
plasma [40] community specifies the average velocity as a scale. A proof is provided here that the only scale
that separates the linear and nonlinear sources of uncertainty is the value at which the derivative of the
log-log of the distribution is negative one. Given the connection to the surprisal of the distribution, this will
be referred to as the informational scale.
    Recall that a shape-scale distribution can be scaled if given a standard (non-scaled) random variable,
X ∼ f (x), the scaled random variable is σX ∼ σ1 f ( σx ). While this is a criterion for the distribution, it does
not specify the definition of the scale, since σ ′ = aσ also satisfies the relationship.

of the universality classes (κ, α) for nonlinear, complex systems. A proof is provided that samples from a
CSED with κ ̸= 0 should be regarded as occupying a fractal information dimension. Furthermore, in the
applications section on thermodynamics, we will see that this same information dimension is occupied by
a nonlinear generalization of the system’s internal energy. The information dimension is used to raise a
distribution to a power and renormalize. This independent-equals distribution represents the distribution of
a fractal number of samples rather than one, thereby accounting for the long-range dependence between
non-exponential samples.

    The specification of the information dimension for nonlinear systems provides the proof regarding the
requirement for the entropy function 24 and its principal constraint to be an average over the independent-
equals moment. This also resolves that the root 1/α of the coupled logarithm must be outside the average;
otherwise the moment would be over x instead of xα .
    With these foundations regarding the precision with which the scale of a shape-scale distribution must
be defined and measured, we can now turn to the calibrated definition for entropy. Central to the approach
will be a recognition that entropy is not simply a mathematical abstraction, but a physical measurement of
a system’s degrees of freedom.

Physical definition, axioms, and theorem for entropy
Shannon [24] and Khinchin [17] established their, now famous, “properties” for defining the entropy function.
Later, these were canonized as the “axioms” for continuity,   maximality, extensibility, and additivity, which
were proven to be satisfied by the entropy function, − i pi ln pi , established by Gibbs and Boltzmann [41].
                                                       P
In turn, investigations into extending entropy to nonlinear, complex systems have focused on modifying the
fourth axiom, with the assumption that a well-designed axiom would provide the basis for a uniqueness proof


                                                                                     8
regarding alternative functions. In recent years, Hanel-Thurner [23] provided a comprehensive expression for
trace entropy functions that satisfy the first three axioms, and Tempesta [22] provided a general axiom based
on a universal group composition. The unintended consequence of canonizing Shannon-Khinchin axioms
is that these mathematical properties disconnected the definition of entropy from the measurement of a
physical property.
    I propose to address this lack of physicality in proposed entropy functions by focusing on the physical
measurements instrumental to entropy, and thereby prove that the establishment of a common unit of
measure across all complex universality classes leads to a unique function for entropy. The proposed definition
and axioms emphasize clarity regarding the important assumptions, rather than a minimal foundation.
Future research could certainly trim these axioms and strengthen the theorems; however, given the proposed
calibration, the definition of entropy will not change. While it may be possible to extend the definition, it
will not be easy to do so. An investigator seeking an alternative measure of entropy will now have to a)
prove that the Hanel-Thurner classification of complex systems was not comprehensive and/or b) prove that
the independent scale is not the unique variable quantifying the linear uncertainty, independently of the
nonlinearity.

Thermodynamic and Information Applications
Having established the unique, universal function that fulfills a calibration to measure entropy, a plethora
of applications will benefit from the improved analytical precision. Central to these applications will be
the establishment of a model for NESS systems. To date, proposals for a NESS model have had difficulty
defining a consistent temperature, from which entropy, energy, and the partition function can have a clear
relationship. After introducing definitions for temperature, internal energy, and free energy, its application
to information systems is described.
    Boltzmann famously discovered the microscopic principles for computing the macroscopic property of
entropy before the discovery of the atom. Gibbs extended entropy to probabilistic analysis of systems. The
Nonlinear Statistical Coupling now provides a framework for the thermodynamics of nonequilibrium steady
state (NESS) systems [43–45]. Remarkably, this framework splits the macroscopic free energy (physics) and
the microscopic free energy (information) into separate functions connected via a hyperbolic geometry. Thus,
Amari’s insights regarding the duality of information geometry are key to connecting the thermodynamics
and information theory of nonlinear systems. The starting point for this analysis is the requirement that the
temperature is defined as the informational scale divided by the Boltzmann constant, T = σ/kB , thereby
assuring its independence from the nonlinear fluctuations, κ. For simplicity, this introduction to the NESS
thermodynamic relationships focuses on a continuous one-dimensional energy model distributed as a coupled
exponential distribution.
    The zeroth, first, and second laws of thermodynamics can be viewed as establishing fundamental defi-
nitions for temperature, energy, and entropy, respectively. The third law of thermodynamics establishes a
minimum entropy at zero degrees Kelvin, which will not be part of this discussion. The laws of thermody-
namics assume equilibrium in part because it’s important that temperature is an intensive property of a
system, while energy and entropy are extensive. Extending these laws to non-equilibrium systems is challeng-
ing. For instance, the fluctuations associated with non-equilibrium make it unclear how a single temperature
can be defined.
    Nevertheless, physicists have sought approaches for defining the intensive and extensive properties of
non-equilibrium steady-state systems (NESS). Such a generalization has been a principal objective of the
research in nonextensive physics [46,47]. However, despite significant progress [48–51] there remain difficulties
in defining a consistent generalized temperature [52] that supports a full thermodynamic framework. I
will show that a consistent temperature, independent of the system’s nonlinearity, can be defined using
the informational scale constraint. Given the independence of the generalized temperature from the non-
equilibrium fluctuations, a generalized zeroth law of thermodynamics is formulated. This result motivates
further research into understanding the transitivity properties of this definition of temperature, and how the
definition can be extended to systems with different coupling values.

Definition 4 (Generalized Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics) Let systems A, B, and C each possess the same nonlinear
statistical coupling, κ. If, under weak thermal contact, A and C reach a joint non-equilibrium steady state, and B
and C also reach a joint non-equilibrium steady state, then A and B, when placed under weak thermal contact, will
reach a joint non-equilibrium steady state with the same κ.

    A consistent generalization of thermodynamics is crucial to establishing a coherent model for informa-
tion and intelligence in non-equilibrium environments. The optimization of the coupled free energy can
advance the design of variational inference for extreme environments, impact the active inference model of
neuroscience, and improve the design of congested communication systems.
    Within neuroscience, the predictive coding model [55, 56] is a leading candidate for explaining the effi-
ciency and effectiveness of biological intelligence. The model is based on local updates at each neuron, in
which error signals from lower layers are compared with predictions from higher layers, with the difference
generating new error and prediction signals. Nevertheless, within artificial intelligence, global signaling via
backpropagation [57] dominates the training of deep learning algorithms [56,58]. Variational inference [59], in
which probabilistic models are learned and utilized for generative and discriminative capabilities, may be a
bridge between these two paradigms. Variational inference, including predictive coding, turns the intractable
problem of learning an unknown posterior distribution into an optimization problem of approximating that
distribution given a parameterized family of distributions. The negative of the evidence lower bound of the
optimization is equivalent to the free energy and consists of a negative log-likelihood, which is the error
signal between a generated and original dataset, and the divergence between the posterior and prior latent
distribution, which is a regulator maintaining model simplicity.
    Expanding the scope of variational inference to non-exponential distributions could be crucial to improv-
ing neurological models and accelerating the capability of artificial systems [60]. Such methods could
be deployed in the heavy-tailed domain to improve inference robustness [61] or in the compact-support
domain [62] to improve efficiency via spareness. Efforts to utilize the Rényi [63], Tsallis [64], and an initial


                                                         19
implementation with the coupled entropy [61], were limited to the domain of finite variance (κ < 1) to ensure
convergence of the training. Recently, the coupled Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) [65] was designed to
draw samples from the independent-equals modification to the latent coupled Gaussian distribution, thereby
guaranteeing that even the most extreme heavy-tailed distributions with κ ≫ 1 could be trained. This is
because the coupling and scale of the latent distribution are transformed by 1+κκ
                                                                                   and √1+κ
                                                                                         σ
                                                                                             , respectively. This
design demonstrated improvements of 10% and 25% in measures of Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similar-
ity and Multiscale Structural Similarity, respectively, across coupling values ranging from 10−5 to 105 . The
code base for the nonlinear statistical coupling methods and the CVAE algorithm is referenced in Methods
subsection Data and Code Availability.
    A further development in neuroscience is the extension of variational to active inference [66], by which
planning, decision-making, and actions are incorporated into the same theoretical framework. Traditionally,
decisions and actions have been modeled as optimizing utility functions. An important example of this is the
reinforcement algorithm in which an optimal expected utility is chosen from a variety of plans. Friston [67]
showed that normalization of the utility is equivalent to a prior distribution weighting an agent’s preferences.
An agent, such as a cell, is separated from its environment, such as a fluid host, by a Markov Blanket [68],
the cell membrane. Importantly, the Markov Blanket must provide a conditionally independent boundary
between the agent and its surroundings; however, this condition assumes equilibrium. The coupled free
energy function enables the definition of a pseudo-Markov Blanket in which non-equilibrium fluctuations
that transverse the boundary could be included in the model. The transition from an exponential equilibrium
model to a non-exponential non-equilibrium model would still require that the agent/environment cross
terms of the correlation matrix be zero, but now a nonlinear dependence between the boundary would be
permitted. While developed for neuroscience, the active inference model is a general intelligence model that
can be applied to variety of agent types navigating turbulent environments.
    In parallel with the complexity of AI systems, modern communications systems, such as 5G wireless and
cognitive radio networks, are facing increased complexity as dense traffic creates heavy-tailed interference.
In contrast to a Gaussian noise channel, interference fluctuates in intensity depending on the instantaneous
use of the channel. For example, Clavier et al. measured [69] tail shapes above κ > 2, in 5G networks,
indicative of quite extreme fluctuations. In cognitive radio designs [70], secondary users must adaptively
switch to open channels to guarantee non-interference with primary users. These applications cannot use
standard metrics such as the mean-square error or the Shannon entropy, since the outliers invalidate the
metrics’ foundational assumptions.
    To date, the Rényi function has been the most common generalized entropy function used in information
systems [71–73]. The probability analysis of the entropy function presented in Methods subsection Pro-
posed entropy functions provides insight regarding why the Rényi function, despite being non-optimal, is
nevertheless effective in these circumstances. A high-interference channel characterized by a shape between
1 < κ/2 < 2, such as the distributions shown in Figure 6, has a Shannon entropy measure dominated by the
nonlinear DOF. The Rényi function dramatically reduces this influence if its index matches this tail shape
via q(κ, α, d) = 1 + α+dκ
                       ακ
                           . The calibrated entropy framework is expected to further improve communication
design in the presence of heavy-tailed noise in two ways. First, the connection between multiplicative noise,
heavy-tailed tail shape, and the index of the coupled entropy is made explicit, which will make the design
choices more explicit. Second, the full discounting of the nonlinear DOF requires the calibrated entropy.

entropy

a correction to the q-exponential distribution by raising it to a power q, which does correct the definition
of temperature but also disassociates the distribution with the proposed function for entropy. Both of these
issues are evident in the attempt to associate turbulence with a two-parameter function for entropy.

Discussion
Entropy, computed correctly, is a precise, calibrated measurement of a system’s degrees of freedom. That
definition must be accurate across all complex, nonlinear systems, which are comprehensively specified by
four parameters:

 • κ - the functional nonlinearity that creates long-range dependence
 • α - the variable nonlinear stretch that creates short-range dependence
 • d - the number of dimensions of the system
 • σ - the informational scale of the system.

    Proving the uniqueness of the informational scale and its central role in correctly calibrating the mea-
surement of entropy is the principal contribution of this paper. With this insight, the coupled entropy is
proven to be the unique, universal measure of entropy; all other proposed functions fail in some way to
satisfy the three axioms needed to calibrate entropy’s measurement of degrees of freedom:

 • specification of the universality classes (κ, α) (plus dimensions d) for nonlinear, systems which include
   exponential (κ = 0, α = 1), stretched exponential (κ = 0, α > 0), stretched power-law (κ > 0, α > 0),
   and stretched super-exponential (− d+α α
                                              < κ < 0, α > 0);
 • specification of the granularity via the informational scale (σ); and
 • the requirement that calibrated growth of states be based on a four-parameter (including dimensions)
   nonlinear ordinary differential equation.

    This approach to defining entropy is a significant departure from the traditional Shannon-Khinchin
axioms; however, it is truer to the original intentions of Shannon to define entropy as a monotonic measure
(logarithm) of the number of potential messages, and to the efforts of Boltzmann and Gibbs to measure the
degrees of freedom of physical systems. The phrase “axioms” was not used by either Shannon or Khinchin
[17,24]; rather, this term was canonized by scientists seeking to broaden the definition for nonlinear, complex
systems [21, 75]. By focusing exclusively on the mathematical question of composition beyond additivity,
the proposals for a generalized entropy became ungrounded from the physical requirement to measure the
energetic and configurational degrees of freedom.
    Despite the value of a calibrated entropy, this result will not be easy to accept among scientists
accustomed to modifying the Shannon-Khinchin composability axiom to prove an entropy definition. Unfor-
tunately, the logic doesn’t hold that just because additivity led to the correct, calibrated entropy for
equilibrium systems that any non-additive function will be a calibrated measurement of entropy for nonequi-
librium systems. Scientists who’ve taken this approach will not be pleased to learn their favorite function
does not measure entropy. Huxley’s admonishment remains, “The great tragedy of science — the slaying
of a beautiful hypothesis by an ugly fact” [76]. The challenge for those seeking to extend the definition of
entropy presented here is to prove either an extensivity outside the Hanel-Thurner classification and/or a
metric for the degrees of freedom that is more accurate and precise than the informational scale.
    The specification of a particular complexity class can be viewed as a constraint on the nonlinearity of
the system. The BGS entropy is still a relevant measure for all systems, but its count of degrees of freedom
includes the impact of nonlinearity. The purpose of an entropy measure tuned to the complexity class of the
particular system is to discount the nonlinear degrees of freedom and instead count the energetic DOF and
the generalized configurational DOF. That said, a particular nonlinear constraint on the entropy can also be
applied to any system, providing its own unique measurement of the system’s characteristics. The spectrum
of these entropy measurements may encode the full information of a system, though that is a hypothesis
which will have to be evaluated.
    The only function that satisfies the proposed calibration requirement is the coupled entropy with a scaling
and root by the stretching parameter. This calibrated entropy for complex systems has three components:

   1. a generalized logarithm, inverse to the structure of the coupled stretched exponential function;
   2. an average over the independent-equals distribution;
   3. a power outside the average equal to the inverse of the stretching parameter.

(lowering) the cost of outliers and decreasing (increasing) the sampling of outliers for positive (negative)

Maximization and other entropy proofs
A core methodology for information thermodynamics is the derivation of the distribution that maximizes
an entropy function using the Lagrangian function. In this section, the theorem is proven that the coupled
stretched exponential distributions maximizes the coupled entropy given the independent-equals moment
as a constraint. While this theorem focuses on the shape-scale distributions, a variety of other models are

Each of these proposed entropy functions is maximized by the coupled stretched exponential distribution.
The basic properties of these functions are illustrated by their measure of the coupled exponential distribution
(α = 1, d = 1), shown in Figure 6 and Table 4. The limit properties, as the coupling goes to infinity, provide
an important distinction in the function. The Shannon and Rényi entropies approach infinity, as shown in
Figure 6a. The Rényi function does reduce the dependence on the coupling from a linear to a logarithmic
relationship. There are three versions of the Tsallis function that have been explored as potential measures of
entropy. In the figure the q-exponential definition is used which sets the scale to (1+κ)q. The Tsallis function
converges to a constant, raising questions about its ability to be a metric. The normalized Tsallis function
goes to infinity faster than the Shannon entropy, reflective of its instability. The Tsallis conjugate value of
