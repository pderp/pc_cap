                                               RESEARCH REPORT

                                  Generalized Transformers
                            Geometry, Compression, Predictive Coding,
                                     and Continual Learning

                 An architecture for learning systems that keep learning: the framework,
               a working prototype, first experimental evidence, and a plan for scaling up

                  Prepared for Ben Goertzel and colleagues, with ChatGPT assistance
                                                September 18, 2026

                                                     Abstract
          We would like learning systems that keep acquiring knowledge while they are in use without losing
      what they already know, that can hand a newly learned capability to another system as a small
      module that can be checked before it is trusted, that connect naturally to symbolic reasoning, and
      that can build a new representation when the familiar ones fail. Transformers are a strong foundation,
      but fitting the weights of a fixed architecture does not supply these properties by itself. This report
      describes a research program aimed at them, and what its first experiments show.
          The program has four parts. The first is a broader description of what a transformer layer
      does, which we call a generalized transformer (GT). A GT layer decides where to look, brings back
      evidence, makes useful relationships explicit, reconciles competing evidence when necessary, and keeps
      a representation suited to whatever comes next. The description is geometric: the questions one
      can ask of a state define distances between states, routing is a set of input-dependent probability
      weights, and compression is judged by what it does to later behavior. An ordinary transformer block
      is one special case. The second part, compression-driven distinction (CD) networks, is another. In
      a CD network the representation is an executable structure of reusable features together with the
      exceptions those features fail to explain, and a bounded search, guided by a record of earlier searches,
      proposes and tests changes to that structure. The third part concerns how the numerical parameters
      inside such a structure are learned: by ordinary backpropagation (BP), or by predictive coding (PC),
      a family of local, energy-based procedures. The fourth part is a practical route to experiments. The
      same mechanisms can be attached as a cap on top of a pretrained transformer, so that the new
      machinery only has to improve on a model that already works.
          Each idea is presented first in plain language and then mathematically, with worked examples and
      algorithms. We then report a series of small experiments. The strongest cap built so far improves
      next-character prediction on the Tiny Shakespeare corpus substantially, mainly because it gives the
      model a richer memory to read from; structural selection and prediction-time inference add smaller,
      consistent gains. PC learning does not outperform a matched BP control. When the cap is adapted to
      a new domain it forgets a moderate and consistent amount of its earlier skill, and a small replay buffer
      removes a good part of that loss. These results support particular mechanisms and a practical research
      path. They leave open decentralized operation, broad creative generalization, and any advantage at
      frontier scale, and the final part of the report lays out a staged plan, with explicit decision criteria,
      for investigating those questions.


How to read this report. No earlier document on this project is assumed. Section 1 explains the
goals and gives a short overview, and Section 2 reviews the standard ideas the rest depends on. Every
technical section opens with an informal account of its idea before any notation appears, and the running
example of Section 3.2 recurs throughout, so a reader who wants the concepts without the mathematics
can follow those openings and the discussion after each equation. What was built and measured is in
Sections 7–9, and what comes next in Section 10. The appendices hold proofs, exact bounds, full tables,
implementation details and the provenance of every number, and a glossary is at the end.

1 Introduction: why look beyond the standard transformer?

1.1 The starting point

A present-day language model is built in two separate phases. During training, a very large number
of numerical weights are adjusted so that the model predicts text well. During use, those weights are
frozen: the model reads a prompt and answers, and nothing it encounters changes what it knows the
next day. This separation is a large part of why the technology works so well, and it is also the source of
several limitations that become visible as soon as one imagines a system that develops through its own
experience.
A capable conversational model needs a separate training run to turn a useful experience into lasting
knowledge. When its weights are updated on new material, its earlier behavior can deteriorate, sometimes
badly. Passing a newly acquired skill from one model to another usually means running a substantial
adaptation pipeline, because the skill is spread across millions of weights and cannot simply be copied
out. Facts held in a symbolic reasoner are hard to connect to the particular internal computation that
needs them. And a model may produce fluent variations on what it has seen without reliably discovering
a new computational abstraction that would solve an unfamiliar kind of problem.
None of these is an impossibility result about transformers. A transformer can be trained online, given
an external memory, equipped with tools, or embedded in a decentralized system, and existing work on
continual learning, test-time training, retrieval, and parameter-efficient adaptation addresses parts of the
problem [2, 3, 12, 11]. The question pursued here is narrower: whether an architecture that is explicit
about what information it preserves, how new modules are discovered, and how adaptation is kept local
makes these capabilities easier to combine and easier to measure.


1.2 Five goals, and what would count as progress

Five goals motivate the work. For each we say what we mean, and what would and would not count as
achieving it, because several of them are easy to satisfy in a trivial way.


Continual learning with controlled forgetting. The system should learn a new domain while keeping
its useful old behavior. A frozen model does not meet this goal, since it keeps its old skill only by
learning nothing. A lossless archive of everything ever seen does not meet it either if the system cannot
find the relevant evidence when it is needed. We therefore keep three things apart: what knowledge
is stored, whether the system can get at it, and how well it performs when it uses it. The forgetting
experiment in Section 9 isolates a failure of the second kind. The stored knowledge stays fixed, and what
deteriorates is the learned policy for consulting it.


Learning during use. Prediction, short-lived adjustment of internal state, parameter updates, and the
discovery of new structure should be able to coexist, each on its own timescale. An agent should be able
to go on giving useful answers while a slower process investigates an error that keeps recurring. This
does not require every parameter to change during every forward pass. It requires serving and learning
to be parts of one versioned process, with a well-defined order of events and a bound on how much a
running prediction can be disturbed. Predictive coding offers a natural vocabulary for the adjustment of
internal state, but concurrency also depends on scheduling and on how versions and memories are stored.


Decentralized acquisition and exchange of capabilities. Different learners see different data and have
different amounts of compute. We would like them to exchange small executable modules, summaries of

interpreter or planner should be able to supply a typed observation to the same workspace that a neural

should be able to construct and test a new composition or a new representation. A short program that
combines known operators in a way not seen before is one concrete target. Novelty alone is not the
aim: a creative candidate has to help on unseen instances and survive factual or executable checks. A
fixed, finite grammar of operators can be used to test the discovery of new compositions within that
grammar, though it cannot show the invention of entirely new primitives. Benchmarks of compositional
generalization motivate this distinction [5].

1.3 The approach in brief

The report develops four ideas, and they are easier to follow with the whole picture in view.
The first is a general description of what one layer of a transformer-like system does. At each position in
a text the layer works out what it needs, chooses where to look, brings back evidence, computes features
that make useful relationships easy to see, reconciles conflicting evidence if necessary, and writes down a
state for the next layer. We call any layer organized this way a generalized transformer (GT) layer. The

standard transformer block fits the description exactly, with particular choices at each step, and so do
systems that read from dictionaries, run small programs, or keep explicit records. The description is
geometric in a specific sense explained in Section 3: it is built from distances between states, probability
weights over sources, and maps that merge states which need not be told apart.
The second idea is a particular kind of GT layer, the compression-driven distinction (CD) network. A
CD network tries to explain its observations with reusable rules, stores separately the exceptions that
the rules do not cover, and keeps an index so that the right pieces can be found quickly. When errors
recur it searches for a better explanation, such as a new rule or a new combination of old ones, refits the
numerical parts that are affected, and keeps the change if it helps. A record of past searches guides later
ones. The name reflects the two principles at work: good explanations compress the data, and what a
representation must preserve are the distinctions between situations that later computations depend on.
The third idea concerns how the numerical parameters inside such a structure are fitted. Backpropagation
(BP) is the standard method. Predictive coding (PC) is an alternative in which the network first lets
its internal activities settle into a state that balances its expectations against the evidence, and then
adjusts each parameter using only quantities available nearby. We write CD–BP and CD–PC for CD
structures fitted in these two ways.
The fourth idea is practical. A cap is a module attached on top of an existing, largely frozen transformer.
It reads the base model’s internal states and its prediction, consults additional memories or rules, and
outputs a corrected prediction. Any of the mechanisms above can be packaged as a cap, which narrows
the first engineering problem a great deal, because the cap does not have to learn language from scratch.
These four names describe different aspects of a system and can be combined freely, and keeping them
apart is necessary for reading the experiments correctly. GT is a way of organizing the flow of information
and says nothing about the optimizer. A conventional transformer is a GT, a CD architecture is another,
and a CD architecture can contain transformers inside it. CD does not mean the absence of neural
weights. PC is a learning and inference procedure and is distinct from structural search, and a cap trained
with BP can still carry out iterative inference at prediction time. Several experimental comparisons
below differ in exactly one of these respects.


1.4 What has been tested

All experiments so far are small. They run on a CPU-scale setup that we call the sandbox, using a
transformer of about 120,000 parameters that predicts text one byte at a time, mostly on the Tiny
Shakespeare corpus. Their purpose is to find out which mechanisms work and which comparisons are
informative before anything expensive is attempted.
The findings, in brief, are these. Finite program-discovery tasks show that search guided by past
experience finds recurring rules with far fewer trials than unguided search, and that some rules cannot
be found at all unless the system is allowed to test relationships among three or more variables at once.
A series of text experiments shows that the criterion for accepting a new module has a large effect,
and that a strict requirement for a module to pay back its storage cost quickly rejects many useful
corrections. The strongest cap so far, called H2 below, lowers the prediction loss of its base transformer
from about 2.90 to about 2.34 bits per byte. Most of that gain comes from letting the cap read full
probability distributions from a large dictionary of character contexts. Choosing which readers to keep
by a compression-first search, and adjusting trust among readers at prediction time using recent evidence,
each add a smaller gain that is consistent across random seeds. PC and BP give almost identical results
as learning rules. When H2 is adapted to a new domain, it loses between 12 and 14 percent of its
advantage on the old one, and mixing in ten percent of old training examples during adaptation cuts
that loss by about forty percent.

1.5 Relation to existing work

The ingredients have substantial precedents. Judging a compressed representation by what it preserves
for a task connects to the information bottleneck [7]. Learning a library of reusable programs together
with a mechanism that proposes candidates connects to DreamCoder [15]. Memory readers that
supply predictive distributions connect to neural caches and nearest-neighbor language models [13, 12].
The feature-and-residual view of compression comes from work on incremental compression [16], and
the organization of bounded discovery, module reuse and online adaptation from resource-bounded
incremental induction (RBII) [17]. We do not claim that the individual ingredients are new. What is
offered here is a synthesis with explicit interfaces and guarantees, and a small experimental investigation
of it.


2 Background in brief

This section collects the standard ideas used later. It is meant for readers who do not work with language
models day to day, and others can skip it without loss.


Next-token prediction. A language model reads a sequence of tokens and outputs a probability for
every possible next token. Tokens can be words, pieces of words, or single characters. In all the text
experiments below a token is one byte, so there are 256 possible values and the model outputs 256
probabilities at each step. The text the model has already seen is called the prefix or context. A model
is causal, or autoregressive, if its prediction for a position depends only on earlier positions. Much care
in the experiments goes into making sure that no component ever looks, even indirectly, at the token it
is supposed to predict.


Measuring prediction in bits. If the model assigned probability p to the token that did occur, its
loss on that token is − log2 p bits: one bit for probability one half, two bits for one quarter, and so on.
Averaged over a text, this gives the cross entropy. When tokens are bytes the unit is bits per byte (bpb),
and lower is better. A model that knows nothing and spreads its probability evenly over 256 bytes scores
8 bpb. The small transformer used here scores about 2.9 bpb on Shakespeare, and good count-based
models of character sequences reach somewhat lower values. For scale, a change of 0.5 bpb is a large
improvement in this setting and a change of 0.005 bpb is small, though it can still be measured reliably
when it appears in every repetition. The measure is closely related to data compression, since a predictor
with a loss of L bits can in principle be turned into a code that stores the text in about L bits. Section 4
relies on that connection.


The transformer layer. A transformer keeps one vector of numbers for each position in the text and
refines these vectors layer by layer [1]. Each layer has two steps. In the attention step, each position
computes a query vector describing what it is looking for, and every earlier position offers a key vector
describing what it contains and a value vector holding its content. The match between a query and each
key is turned by the softmax function into a set of positive weights that sum to one, and the position
receives the correspondingly weighted average of the values. Several such heads operate in parallel. In
the feed-forward step, a small network is applied separately at each position to the result. The output of
each step is added to its input (a residual connection) and rescaled (normalization).

Training. The weights are fitted by backpropagation: the loss is computed, its derivative with respect to
every weight is obtained by applying the chain rule backward through the whole computation, and each
weight is moved a little in the direction that lowers the loss. The experiments use AdamW, a standard
variant of this gradient descent procedure. BP is global, in the sense that the update to a weight depends
on a signal passed back through everything that lies between that weight and the output.


Count-based predictors. A much older way to predict the next character is to count. An n-gram or
suffix model records, for each short context seen in training text, how often each character followed it,
and predicts in proportion to those counts. When a context was never seen, the model backs off to a
shorter context. Such models are crude in some ways and very strong in others, because they remember
specific strings exactly. They appear below as the readers that a cap consults.


Energy-based inference and predictive coding. In an energy-based network, the internal activities are
found by a search. One defines an energy, a number that is small when every unit’s activity agrees with
what the units feeding into it predict and with the available evidence, and the activities are adjusted
step by step to lower it. This process is called settling. Predictive coding is a family of such models
in which the energy is a sum of squared prediction errors. Learning then uses the settled state: each
parameter is changed according to the prediction error at its own unit, with no backward pass through
the whole network. Under suitable conditions these local updates approximate the gradient that BP
would compute [8, 9].


Minimum description length. The minimum description length (MDL) principle says that the best
explanation of some data is the one that allows the data to be written down most briefly, where the
length of the explanation itself is counted in the total. A rule that covers many cases is worth its cost.
A rule that covers two cases is usually not, because it is cheaper to list the two cases.


3 The generalized transformer

3.1 The idea: six jobs in one layer

Picture each position in a text as a small workspace holding some notes. One layer of processing does
six kinds of work on every workspace.

1. It describes the current need: what question should this workspace answer next?
2. It chooses sources: which earlier positions, memories or modules might hold useful evidence?
3. It brings back evidence from those sources, ideally keeping track of where each piece came from.
4. It exposes relationships, computing features that make a useful relation easy to inspect.
5. It reconciles tentative interpretations when different sources disagree, if that is needed.
6. It prepares the next state, keeping an affordable representation of what later computations will need.

An ordinary transformer does most of this with vectors, attention weights, weighted sums and small
numerical networks. The systems studied in this report are also allowed to use explicit records and
small programs. In both cases the useful question about a workspace is less “what is stored in it?” than
“which differences between situations can the next computation still detect from it?” The rest of this
section makes that question precise.

3.2 A running example

Suppose the text so far is

      The captain is Mira. The cook is Leo. The captain is

and the task is to predict the missing word. We treat whole words as tokens to keep the example
readable, although the experiments use bytes. The six jobs look like this.

1. Need. The context calls for a name associated with “captain.”
2. Routing. A reader that matches phrases finds the earlier, completed phrase “The captain is.” A neural
   attention head could learn a similar notion of relevance.
3. Transport. The reader returns “Mira,” together with the matched phrase or a pointer to its location.
   The earlier name has already been observed, so reading it is legitimate. The missing word itself is out
   of reach.
4. Lift. The layer exposes a feature such as “there is an exact earlier match for this phrase,” together
   with the candidate word to copy, and it keeps the pairing of role with name.
5. Reconcile. If another reader favors “Leo,” the layer uses whatever evidence is available to decide how
   far to trust each. No step of this inference may inspect the missing answer.
6. Re-encode. The layer keeps the candidate distribution and whatever source information and bindings
   the next computation will need. If later queries might include “who is the cook?”, the present question
   about the captain does not justify deleting the cook’s binding.

The output is a probabilistic prediction. Nothing here amounts to a logical guarantee about what an
author will write next. We return to this example several times, and in Section 3.7 we use it to show
why the order of these operations can decide whether information survives.

4 Compression-driven distinction networks

4.1 The idea: rules, exceptions, and a better index

A CD workspace is a small executable explanation of what it has observed. It has three parts: reusable
features or rules, the exceptions those rules fail to explain, and a way of finding the right pieces quickly.
When errors keep recurring, the system tries out a new explanation. The candidate might be a lookup
on the last few characters, a copy rule, a conjunction of two existing features, a relation between them,
or a small composition of known operations. The system refits the numerical parts that the change
affects, checks whether prediction has improved, and keeps the discoveries that help.
The running example shows the range of options. Suppose the system repeatedly sees sentences that
assign names to roles. Storing every sentence preserves all the evidence. Adding an index from each
role to its assigned name makes a later question cheaper to answer. Learning a reusable program, “find
the most recent assignment for this role,” can in some contexts make the index unnecessary. Each
step shortens the description, and a shorter description is an improvement only if it stays accurate and
remains affordable to execute. A detail that one question lets us discard may still be needed by another.
Three earlier lines of work meet in this design. The calculus of distinctions of Section 3 says what a
representation must keep. Incremental compression [16] supplies the view of learning as the repeated
extraction of a feature together with a residual that the feature leaves unexplained. Resource-bounded
incremental induction [17] supplies the organization of bounded search, module reuse and online
adaptation. The definitions needed from each are given below, and no familiarity with those sources is

4.5 Bounded discovery, reuse and adaptation

Following RBII, the design separates three activities. A discovery process proposes executable programs or
fragments of a program graph. A library stores the accepted modules with their definitions, dependencies,
fitted state and the record of where they came from. An execution process selects modules according to
context, applies them, and may adapt them. A reader discovered once does not have to be rediscovered
each time it is needed.
It is convenient to write the state of the whole machine at time t as
                                           Ξt = (Lt , Mt , θt , Ht , It ),

                                                         16
Generalized transformers and CD networks                                                      Research report


where Ht is the record of previous searches. For each observation ot , the system first predicts using Ξt
and the prefix available so far, and only afterwards uses the revealed outcome to update its mutable
state. The order is essential both for coding and for honest evaluation. A system that adapts on the
target and then reports the adapted prediction as if it had been made beforehand has leaked the answer
into its own score.
Updates made to a module in different contexts need not commute, which means that applying two
updates in the opposite order can leave the module in a different state. Sharing a module between
contexts safely therefore requires one of three things: isolating versions, checking that protected behavior
is preserved, or bounding the behavioral difference between the two orders. A bound of the last kind is
useful only if it is measured in a relevant behavioral metric and if its amplification by later computation
is controlled. Appendix A.2 makes this precise. The experiments so far implement only bounded pieces
of this program.


4.6 Dependency towers and a search guide that learns from history

Structural search is expensive, and the design tries to make it cheaper in two ways. It organizes what
the system measures about its data, and it learns from the outcomes of previous searches.
The first device is the dependency tower. Let K describe the state of the data or of the search. Choose
nested families Ok,b of executable observations, indexed by the order k of the dependencies they examine
and by a resource budget b. For observations with bounded real values, let

                                   dk,b (K, K ′ ) = sup |O(K) − O(K ′ )|.                               (16)
                                                 O∈Ok,b

At level (k, b), two states count as different only if some affordable observation of order at most k tells
them apart. A tower might climb from counts of individual features, to interactions between pairs, to
selected interactions among three or more. Order and cost are separate things. A single parity test
involves many variables and is cheap to run, whereas enumerating all pairs over a large vocabulary
involves only two variables at a time and is expensive.
The second device is a search guide, modeled on the support tensor logic machine (STLM), a way of
scoring logical or program templates that still contain unfilled slots without enumerating every way of
filling them. The guide scores a candidate expression e in stages:

                         e 7−→ canon(e) 7−→ Φe (K) 7−→ Ψ(K, e, H) 7−→ Vbϕ (K, e).                       (17)

The expression is first put in a canonical form, so that equivalent ways of writing the same template are
recognized as one, whenever equivalence can be decided within the chosen grammar. It is then evaluated
against the data by Φe , which may be a tensor of predicate values, a selected contraction of such tensors,
or another structured evaluation. Next, Ψ gathers descriptors of the template’s structure, the tower
observations, support counts, statistics of the current residual, the search history and the cost. Finally
a learned regressor Vbϕ , which may be a support-vector machine, a learner over explicit features, or a
neural approximation, estimates how useful the candidate will be.

A useful picture is a mechanic’s notebook. It records which repairs worked under which circumstances,
the unsuccessful ones included. A new fault calls to mind several past cases, so the mechanic begins with
sensible repairs and sensible settings. The repair still has to be carried out and tested. The notebook’s
prediction that a repair will succeed does not certify that the machine now works.
That last distinction is built into the design. The record of verified observations defines the set of states
of the world still compatible with everything established so far,
                      Wt = {K : K satisfies all sound constraints recorded so far}.
Each new sound observation shrinks this set. A predicted outcome can be used to schedule a search
but must never be entered as a proven constraint, and statistical intervals carry their own coverage
assumptions and their own accounting of failures. This separation is what connects the guide to the
exact bounds of Appendix B.
A guide that followed only its own predictions would never discover where they are wrong, so proposals
are drawn from a mixture of the learned distribution qϕ,t and a broad default distribution q0,t :
                                           qt = (1 − εt )qϕ,t + εt q0,t .                                (18)
If a fixed discoverable action has probability at least p > 0 of being proposed on each trial, the probability
that it is missed in n trials is at most (1 − p)n . This elementary bound does not ensure discovery in an
infinite and changing space of programs, and it says nothing about whether refitting will succeed or the
gain will be positive.


4.7 Why higher-order observations are sometimes indispensable

The tower allows for high-order observations because some regularities cannot be seen at any lower
order. Let A and B be independent fair bits and let C = A ⊕ B be their exclusive-or. Any two of the
three variables are statistically independent, even though the three together satisfy a deterministic rule.
Statistics of pairs cannot reveal that rule. The same holds in general: the uniform distributions on the
even-parity and the odd-parity strings in {0, 1}d have identical marginals on every proper subset of
positions, because each assignment to k < d positions has 2d−k−1 completions of either parity.
This motivates a tower that is sparse and selectively deep. The architecture should be able to propose a
compact high-order program when lower-order descriptions fail, without ever building a full joint table
over all variables. Appendix C works through a finite noisy-parity example in which one additional
high-order observation turns an inconclusive compression proposal into a certified improvement.


4.8 Training the guide: warm-up, label quality, and richer features

The guide learns from the outcomes of searches, and the quantity and quality of those outcomes limit
what it can learn. A thousand candidate trials do not necessarily amount to a thousand independent
search situations. Candidates evaluated against the same incumbent share its residuals, its library and
its text. The capacity of the guide should therefore be governed by how many distinct states or search
trajectories support it, and its calibration should be checked on held-out groups of trials, never on
individual trials drawn from the same group. Sparse higher-order features of the guide can be shrunk
toward a simpler guide until the data support them.

5 Caps: attaching CD to an existing transformer

5.1 The idea

A cap is a specialist workspace attached to a model that is already capable. The base model reads the
text and supplies a default prediction. The cap consults additional memories or rules and decides whether
they justify a correction. The new mechanism can then concentrate on the base model’s remaining errors
and on new knowledge, and does not have to learn language from the beginning.
In the running example, the base might supply a plausible distribution over names. A copy reader notices
that the phrase “The captain is” was completed earlier in the text, and the cap uses that evidence to

raise the probability of Mira. When the memory has nothing relevant to offer, a well-trained cap should
leave the base prediction almost unchanged. Whether it makes that decision correctly has to be tested,
since neither a small parameter count nor a frozen base guarantees it.

6 Learning inside the structure: predictive coding and backpropagation

6.1 The idea: revise the answer, the habit, or the machinery

A learner can respond to an error in three different ways. It can revise a temporary interpretation:
perhaps the copy reader deserves more trust on this particular prefix. It can revise a persistent habit:
perhaps its gate should generally trust that reader in circumstances like these. Or it can revise the
available machinery: perhaps it needs a new reader, or a new relation between existing features.
The three components of the design correspond to these three responses. Predictive coding separates
temporary activities from slowly learned parameters, and uses discrepancies between prediction and
evidence to settle the activities. CD supplies the process that revises structure. The persistent parameters
can be fitted by PC’s local rule, or by BP applied to the same computation. The research question is
which combination gives the best trade-off among prediction, retention, discovery and compute. The
existence of a local update formula is no evidence that PC learns better than BP. That has to be
measured against a matched BP control, and Sections 8 and 9 report such measurements.

7 The working cap: a concrete generalized-transformer instance

Two hybrid caps have been built. The first, H1, was a small neural cap whose results were instructive
but negative, and it is described with those results in Section 8.6. This section describes its successor,
H2, which is the working system behind the main results and the forgetting study. H2 is deliberately
simple. It has a frozen base transformer, a bank of memory readers that each propose a full next-byte
distribution, and a small trainable gate that decides how far to trust each reader in the current context.


7.1 Readers that report full distributions

The base is a frozen transformer with two blocks, width 64, four attention heads and a context of 64

a complete distribution over the next byte, and all share the same uniform floor, so that no byte ever

7.3 Choosing the structure: which readers to keep

Around this gate, H2 compares three strategies for choosing the structure. The fixed architecture uses
five readers chosen in advance. Forward CD search starts from a small set and adds readers, ranking
each candidate by the improvement it brings when inserted into the mixture and then refitting within a
bounded budget. Compression-first CD search fits all 13 readers and then removes readers until five
remain, refitting the candidate removals and their controls along the way.
Compression-first search consumes 1,056 optimizer updates in all, counting the trials that are rejected:
240 initial updates, then eight rounds of removal, each with two candidates and one control at 24 updates
apiece, then 240 final updates. Each removal is selected under a constraint that forces the number of
readers down, so it need not improve on the unconstrained full ensemble. The base reader always remains
available. The fixed-graph controls that are matched to this search receive 1,056 ordinary updates. Equal
numbers of updates do not amount to equal floating-point work.
A separate transfer condition takes the five-reader graph found in development and simply refits its gate.
This tests the reuse of one search result, and is no evidence about a trained search guide generalizing
across domains. The ensemble of all 13 readers serves as the reference for accuracy. H2 optimizes
predictive quality under a budget on active readers. It does not compute the ideal sufficient quotient of
Section 4, and it does not demonstrate that its code cost is repaid in full.

7.4 H2 as a generalized transformer

Each part of H2 corresponds to a GT operation. The suffix and copy lookups carry out source selection
and transport. The reader distributions expose predictive distinctions. The hidden state and the reader
statistics form the local product and its features. Equation (31) is the reconciliation step. The normalized
mixture is the output re-encoding. Structural CD changes which reader coordinates are active, and PC
or BP changes the gate parameters. Because the parts are separable in this way, each can be removed or
replaced to measure its contribution, which is how the comparisons in the next section are made.
H2 is a restricted cap. The transformer and the dictionary are frozen, the grammar offers only 13 readers,
and the only numerical plasticity is a small gate. These restrictions make the evidence cheap to obtain
and straightforward to interpret. They also limit what can be concluded about a complete, standalone
CD learner.


8 Experimental evidence

8.1 How to read these experiments

The experiments form a research sequence, and they should not be read as a single benchmark in which
every system was held to identical conditions. The sequence began with finite program-discovery tasks
on synthetic binary data. It continued through six successive text prototypes of CD, referred to below as
v1 to v6, which began as standalone predictors and became caps on frozen base models. It then moved

8.3 Early text prototypes

The first text prototype, v1, showed that a standalone finite CD predictor could operate autoregressively
on real text. Guided CD scored 3.3132 bpb, better than a bigram model’s 3.5862, while an interpolated
4-gram model scored 2.6523. The transformer base in this pilot had been trained for only 300 steps and
scored 3.8454. Both a CD cap and a conventional neural cap improved on it. With a base that weak the
result could say little about caps on a competent model, and it prompted a control with a stronger base.
The second prototype, v2, trained the transformer for 2,500 steps. Its CD cap selected no additions at
all and stayed at the base score of 2.894651 bpb, while a conventional neural cap reached 2.888592. A
CD cap on the 4-gram base also selected nothing, and historical guidance did worse than simpler choices.
The mechanism ran correctly. What failed was the admission rule: the strict short-horizon model-cost
criterion discussed in Section 4.4 filtered out corrections that might have helped.

8.6 Hybrid H1: a useful negative result

H1 was the first cap to combine CD structure search with PC learning. It attached a nonlinear cap to
the same small frozen transformer later used by H2. The cap had 32,064 allocated parameters, a finite
grammar of observers, parameter fitting by either PC or BP, growth of structure by CD, screening of
candidates by residuals, and a small history guide. The main test scores use a blend of base and cap
whose weight was selected on development data. Table 6 also shows the raw cap scores, because the
blending changed the ranking of PC against BP.
The simplest condition did best. The fixed BP cap beat CD–PC in every one of the three seeds. CD–PC
beat CD–BP by only 0.000338 bpb on average after blending, and the order of the two reversed for the
raw caps. Historical guidance had essentially no effect on deployed performance and slightly worsened
an audit of structural choices under PC. Diagnostics in which a reader’s features were erased while the
rest of the cap stayed frozen did show that the readers added by search contributed to prediction, but
not that the grown architecture had any advantage over a fixed architecture trained afresh.
One thing H1 did establish is that the PC machinery computes what it is meant to compute. Its updates
approached BP gradients as the nudge was made smaller and settling longer, as Theorem 6.1 predicts.

the next cap: a richer evidence interface, and a form of prediction-time inference that does some useful
work of its own, since additional PC sweeps alone had achieved little.
A later audit evaluated the long fixed controls every 48 updates. Table 6 had evaluated them only at
round boundaries. With the same training budget of 1,560 updates, and with the checkpoint selected on
validation data, the test means were 2.878186 bpb for BP and 2.877808 for PC, and every seed selected
update 168. This audit of checkpoint policy likewise found no consistent advantage for structural PC
learning.

8.9 What the experiments support so far

The evidence favors a strong and explicit reader interface, the evaluation of structural choices by their
measured effect on the deployed predictor, keeping useful teachers when students are distilled from them,
a history-guided search whose outcome labels are calibrated, and a modest amount of prediction-time
inference over recent evidence. It equally favors keeping simple controls in every comparison: ordinary
numerical fitting, screening by residuals alone, the full reader bank, and direct reuse of a graph.
The evidence does not establish that rich tower features always help, that PC is a better parameter learner
than BP, that more search always improves prediction, or that a standalone CD network can compete
with a large, well-trained transformer. The best empirical result to date is a small memory-assisted
cap. Its value for the program is as an intermediate target whose components can each be tested
independently.


9 Forgetting: what happens when the cap learns something new?

Continual learning is the first goal of Section 1, and H2 allows a direct if small test of it. We take a cap
trained on Shakespeare, continue training it on a different kind of text, and measure two things: how
much it learns about the new text, and how much worse it becomes on Shakespeare.


9.1 Protocol, and what is meant by forgetting

Training was continued from the five H2 checkpoints produced by compression-first PC search. Each
was adapted independently to two new domains, Alice in Wonderland and Python source code. For
every domain and seed, three arms each received 1,024 updates: PC, BP, and PC with 10% replay. Five
further PC runs continued training on Shakespeare alone, as a control for the effect of simply running the
optimizer longer. That makes 35 adaptation and control runs. Along their trajectories 595 checkpoints
were saved, and all were hashed before any held-out evaluation took place.
The base, the reader dictionary, the selected graph of readers and the feature normalization all stayed
frozen. Only the gate was trainable, and it continued from its original checkpoint together with the
saved state of its AdamW optimizer. PC and BP received the same minibatches of new-domain data,
and both used the same inference at prediction time. In the replay arm, 26 of the 256 examples in each
minibatch were replaced by samples from a fixed buffer of 1,024 examples of old training data. This is
10.16% replay, and it means slightly less exposure to new data for the same number of updates.

9.3 Why frozen readers can coexist with forgetting

It may seem odd that a system whose memory is frozen forgets anything. The explanation lies in
Equation (21). The readers qj stay fixed, but the weights wj (x; θ) that decide how far each is trusted do
change. An update on the new domain can leave every stored count untouched and still change how the
old evidence is used. The present system retains the availability of its knowledge more firmly than it
retains effective access to it, which is the distinction drawn under the first goal in Section 1.
An analogy may help. The library still has all the old books. The librarian, however, has picked up new
habits about which shelf to consult, and now sends an old question to the wrong shelf more often than
before. Glancing at how recent questions turned out helps the librarian reconsider, which is the role
settling plays. Replay gives the librarian practice at answering old questions while learning the new job.
Settling at prediction time does provide some protection, without removing the effect. With the same
frozen checkpoints from before and after adaptation, switching settling off raises the estimated forgetting
from 0.065971 to 0.070766 bpb for Alice and from 0.079041 to 0.083149 for Python. This diagnostic
changes inference only, and is a different thing from retraining a model without PC. As learning rules,
PC and BP behave almost identically throughout the main adaptation comparison.
The appropriate engineering response is explicit protection of retention: replay, probes that guard the
routing of protected examples, separate retained gates or branches, growth of modules under version
control, and cautious consolidation. Of these only replay has been measured. The others are hypotheses,
and they call for the same paired tests of retention and plasticity.


9.4 What can be guaranteed about retention

Some retention guarantees are available, and it helps to be clear about what they cover. They concern
complete predictors that are kept and protected. A frozen component inside a predictor that continues
to change enjoys no such guarantee.
Suppose the deployed predictor is a fixed mixture of a retained old predictor and a new one,

                          psafe (y | x) = ρpold (y | x) + (1 − ρ)pnew (y | x),     ρ > 0.

9.5 The continual-learning question that remains

The present test covers a small gate over frozen evidence and a fixed structure, two independent transfers,
and a finite number of updates. It does not test a long stream of tasks, updates to the reader memory,
deletion of old modules, or joint training of the base. A stronger study would combine repeated returns
to earlier domains, a fixed total memory budget, selective growth and retirement of modules, and
comparisons among replay, retained gates and regularization. It would have to measure new learning
as carefully as retention, so that a conservative model cannot appear to succeed merely by refusing to
change.


10 Scaling from the sandbox toward frontier systems

10.1 What survives approximation, and what does not

The ideal operations of Sections 3–6 specify what a useful computation should accomplish, and several
of them cannot be afforded as algorithms. Computing an exact sufficient quotient can require answering
an enormous family of future questions. Joint dependency tables grow exponentially with the number of
variables. Program search branches combinatorially. Refitting everything globally can cost more than
the prediction it improves is worth. And retaining every useful model eventually exceeds any finite
memory.
The practical program is to hold on to a small number of measurable capabilities while replacing each
expensive ideal by a controlled approximation. Table 9 pairs each ideal with a scalable approximation
and with the check that the approximation makes necessary. The cap route is the nearer-term target,
because a strong base supplies broad competence in language, the read and write interfaces can be fixed,
and many candidate trials can share the base’s computations. One caution applies. A stronger base
leaves smaller and noisier residual gains for a cap to find, so success on a weak base is not enough.

10.4 The vocabulary and reader bottleneck

H2 can afford to materialize 13 distributions over 256 bytes at every position. With the vocabulary of a
large language model in place of 256 bytes, the same naive approach becomes costly. Three changes are
plausible.

1. Readers return sparse distributions, or sparse deviations from a shared fallback, and only a few
   readers are activated at each position.
2. Shared context statistics are cached, and compatible reader operations are batched on an accelerator,
   with no Python loops over tokens or graph nodes.
3. The base’s exact probabilities are reused for every vocabulary item a correction leaves untouched,
   and normalizers are computed without discarding the tail of the distribution.

The third point can be made exact. If the correction score s(y) of Equation (19) is zero outside a finite
set S of tokens, the normalizer is

confidence and retrieval features may also be useful, but their accuracy has to be checked, because errors

Sparse mixture-of-experts systems are an engineering precedent for activating only part of a large model

10.5 Predictive coding at scale: keep the useful inference, and charge for the settling

The experiments show a small benefit from reconciling the trust in readers with recently observed
evidence. They do not show a cheaper learning rule, and in H1’s CPU reference implementation PC
learning was much slower than BP. A scaling study should therefore keep three questions separate:
whether activity inference improves quality, whether PC learning enables useful modularity or retention,
and whether either repays its cost.
Practical approximations include good warm starts for the activities, a small learned predictor of the
settled state, stopping when the residual falls below a tolerance, settling only on uncertain cases, and
updating only a small active set of modules. The learned predictor can be distilled from a slower teacher,
with the difficult cases sent back to explicit inference. When the energies are nonconvex some criterion of
stability is needed, since descending the energy does not certify that the resulting prediction is globally
good.
No method is cheapest as a matter of course. BP through unrolled inference pays in memory for stored
activations. Implicit gradients need assumptions about the solver. PC needs extra settling and local
state. Before a result is attributed to the learning rule, the methods compared should have the same
access to readers, the same memory and the same prediction-time inference.

10.6 Decentralization as the exchange of modules

Local gradient rules are sometimes presented as the route to decentralized learning. The proposal here
is different: what the learners exchange is modules. A portable module record might contain

   m = (typed ports, program/weights, version, dependencies, evidence, uncertainty, resource profile).

A worker publishes a candidate module together with examples from its search. A worker that receives
it checks the compatibility of its interface, checks whether its information duplicates what the recipient
already has, measures the gain on its own new tasks, and measures any regression on its old ones
(Figure 4). Evidence summaries must record which examples or sources contributed to them, so that
copied evidence is not later mistaken for independent support. Workers whose data differ may quite
rationally keep different modules.
The quantities to measure are predictive transfer per communicated byte, the time taken to reach
useful local adaptation, the number of duplicate discoveries avoided, and the damage done by stale or
incompatible modules. A common shared embedding would be convenient, though symbolic ports and
small adapters may allow bases of different kinds to take part. Exact semantic equivalence of arbitrary
programs cannot in general be decided, and typed contracts with bounded probes are the practical
substitute. No decentralized experiment has yet been run in this project.


10.7 Linking to symbolic systems, and testing creativity

The GT interface gives other AI systems a concrete point of entry. A PLN query could return a
distribution together with its supporting evidence. A MeTTa program could compute a reader. A
planner could propose a continuation. An episodic store could return a case with a record of its origin.
The gate, or a shared workspace, then decides how each piece of evidence affects the prediction. Typed
ports should distinguish among a fact, a probabilistic belief, an executable rule and a proposed hypothesis.
To test creativity, one should hold out whole families of problems, going beyond new rows of familiar
text. The tests should separate new bindings, new combinations of known operators, new depths
of dependency, and libraries of operators that have truly been extended. Generated rules should be
validated by execution or by external evidence, and then tested for reuse on separate problems. CD
search should be compared with a strong conventional model given the same external tools and the same
compute at test time, so that mere access to more tools is not mistaken for an architectural advantage.
One intermediate experiment would be especially informative. It would combine text with explicit
relational episodes: assignments of roles, updates over time, exceptions and counterfactual questions.
The same facts can be presented in different wording, while the answer requires a new composition of

Generalized transformers and CD networks                                                    Research report


them. One would measure prediction, the recovery of executable rules, the cost of search, and later
transfer. This connects the binding example of Section 3.7 to a tractable test of a neural–symbolic
capability, and avoids the pretense that a lower loss on Shakespeare measures creativity.


10.8 What would justify a frontier-scale claim?

A cap would earn such a claim by showing reliable gains on strong bases, across independent domains,
across streams of old and new tasks, and across combinations of modules, with memory and latency fully
accounted for. A standalone replacement for the transformer would need, in addition, a competitive
scaling curve for learning representations from raw data. Several model sizes and data budgets, at the
least, are needed to distinguish a fixed advantage at small scale from a trend that is sustained.
The most promising working hypothesis is that dense learned representations remain an efficient substrate,
while explicit reusable readers, local activity inference and history-guided structural search take over
some forms of adaptation and reasoning. Whether the gains would eventually justify replacing substantial
parts of the dense network is an empirical question. The present evidence makes progressively stronger
caps a concrete way of investigating it.


11 Assessment and research priorities

The generalized transformer provides a common geometric language for transformer blocks, executable
distinctions, memory readers and iterative inference. CD adds a principled place for structural discovery,
refitting, compression and accumulated search experience. PC and BP are two ways of learning the
numerical parameters inside that architecture. Caps make the whole program experimentally accessible,
by retaining a competent base and narrowing what the new mechanism has to learn.
The experiments indicate a credible immediate direction. Readers should keep their full predictive
content. Structure should be judged by its measured contribution to the deployed predictor. Strong
fixed-graph and full-bank controls should accompany every comparison. And the learned routing policy
should be protected during adaptation. Guidance by search history remains promising, particularly
where motifs recur and outcome labels are precise, but added complexity in the guide should have
to earn its place on independent trajectories. The observed benefit of PC, so far, lies more clearly in
reconciling internal state with recent evidence at prediction time than in any superiority as a rule for
learning parameters.
The next priorities are replication on fresh corpora, longer streams testing retention and plasticity under
a fixed memory budget, stronger base models under the caps, and direct measurement of how well the
cost of structural search is amortized. A parallel symbolic track should test typed program readers and
held-out compositions. Decentralized exchange of modules, and bases prepared jointly with their caps,
are natural later extensions. The intended outcome is a learner that can preserve, access, revise and share
useful structure throughout its operation. The results reported here establish some first mechanisms
toward that outcome, and the greater part of the program is still ahead.

Every distribution includes a uniform smoothing floor with ϵ = 1/256, applied as p 7→ (1 − ϵ)p + ϵ/256,
and the copy mixtures preserve the same floor. The dictionary retains contexts with support of at least
two, up to 16,000 per depth for depths 1–8, chosen by support with deterministic tie-breaking. Backoff
from the shortest contexts starts from a smoothed unigram distribution.
The gate’s four features per reader are the entropy divided by log 256, the peak probability, log(1 +
support)/10, and the probability the reader assigns to the base’s most likely byte. Means and standard
deviations from the fitting set are used for normalization, with standard deviations floored at 0.05
and normalized coordinates clipped to [−8, 8]. The feature coordinates of inactive readers are masked.
Temporary activities are reinitialized for every prefix, so no inference state persists across evaluated
examples.
The predefined five-reader graph contains the base, suffix depths 2, 4 and 6 at strength 4, and copy
length 2. The graph transferred from development contains the base, suffix depth 2 at strength 4, the
copy/suffix reader of length 3, suffix depth 6 at strength 16, and copy length 2. The five compressed PC
graphs vary by seed. Most retain the base, suffix depth 8 at strength 4, suffix depth 3 at strength 16,
copy length 2 and the copy/suffix reader of length 3. Seed 19 uses copy length 3, and seed 71 includes
suffix depth 2 at strength 4 in place of suffix depth 3 at strength 16.

D.2 Forgetting data and replay

The Alice text is Project Gutenberg ebook 11 [24]. Boilerplate was removed and typographic punctuation
was normalized to ASCII. The processed file has 144,862 bytes, with split boundaries at 0, 101,403,
123,132 and 144,862. Each region supplies only those targets whose complete 64-byte prefix lies inside
the region.
Python training uses argparse.py, ast.py, configparser.py, csv.py, enum.py, fnmatch.py,
functools.py, heapq.py and pickle.py, 452,358 bytes in all. Validation uses difflib.py, 83,368
bytes. Testing uses fractions.py, pathlib.py, random.py, statistics.py, textwrap.py and
tokenize.py, 215,403 bytes. The exact Python 3.12 sources, their license and their hashes are kept in
the experiment archive.
The replay buffer consists of 1,024 examples from the original gate-training data, sampled separately
for each seed. Cached features and reader likelihoods remain valid for replay because the base, the
dictionary and the normalization are all frozen. If any of those changed, the cache would have to be
recomputed, or raw contexts replayed instead. Held-out targets are never replayed. The old-task test
segments were scored only after all 35 runs had finished and their checkpoints had been locked.

F Provenance of the evidence, verification and reproduction

F.1 Where the numbers come from

No language model was trained in the course of writing this report. It brings together the theory with
experiments that had already been run and archived, and every number in it comes from the project’s
experiment records. The main tables for H1, H2, v6 and forgetting are generated from machine-readable
analysis files kept with the source of this document. The numbers for the finite tasks and for CD text
v1–v5 are transcribed from the project’s methodology report, and those experiments were not rerun.
The source bundle contains the complete ASCII LaTeX with embedded diagrams, plots and bibliography,
the code that generates the tables, a compact script that checks the numbers, and the JSON and
Markdown analysis files behind the principal tables. It does not duplicate all the model checkpoints and
corpora in the experiment archives. The LaTeX file has its tables and figure coordinates embedded, so it
compiles independently wherever the required TeX packages are installed. The standard algorithmicx
and algpseudocode package files are included for convenience.

F.2 The scope of the recorded validation

The finite prototype reported 18 tests and exact round trips of code packets. CD v6 reported 14 imple-
mentation tests, 12 round trips through an arithmetic coder using real models, 6,291,456 causal checks
of prefixes and branches, and 10,734 matched-root records. These counts document the implementation
work that was recorded. They are not independent statistical replications.
H1 reported nine checks. They covered the derivatives of the energy, convergence of the weak nudge,
descent during settling, growth that preserves the computed function and the continuity of the optimizer,
causal prediction and readers, the algebra of screening, soft targets, and bitwise-identical continuation.
H2 reported seven mathematical, causal and checkpoint checks, an audit of gradients and settling in the
trained state, and the recomputation of 450 means.
The forgetting experiment added three checks: that the data roles are disjoint, that replay is drawn only
from old training targets, and that training which is interrupted and resumed is bitwise equivalent to
uninterrupted training. Its audit recomputed 1,126 loss means and verified all 595 checkpoint hashes,
the initial gates, the frozen base, the dictionary and the fingerprints of the training sources. During
an earlier audit, some H2 diagnostic loss files had to be regenerated by running inference alone from
a locked checkpoint. No model was refitted, the previously recorded means were recovered, and the
original archive records the repair.


F.3 A suggested sequence for replication

1. Recompute the tables from the bundled JSON and check the illustrative coding arithmetic. Compile
   this document and confirm that the generated table values agree.

2. Use the H2 and forgetting archives to rebuild the omitted feature caches, and rerun their locked
   recipes in a fresh results directory, keeping the original evidence separate.
3. Repeat the fixed recipe on fresh corpora before any further tuning. Include a strong base, a fixed
   cap, the full reader bank, CD–BP, CD–PC, and controls without settling and with replay or retained
   branches.
4. For studies of the guide, split the data by search trajectory as well as by text, keep the negative

   and search costs.
5. Increase scale only after independent gains survive matching of compute and memory. Treat the goals

G Glossary


  Term                        Meaning in this report
  Observer                    A function that returns a specified fact, feature or predictive distribution
                              from a state.
  Distinction                 A difference between states that an observer can detect.
  Pseudometric                A distance-like function that can assign zero distance to internally
                              different states.
  Coupling                    Input-dependent routing weights from a destination to its allowed
                              sources; each row sums to one.
  Tagged measure              A representation of selected payloads with their weights and source
                              identities, before aggregation.
  Lift                        The addition of derived coordinates while the original state is retained.
  Continuation                A declared later use of a representation, including the relevant
                              downstream computation.
  Behavioral quotient         The summary obtained by identifying states that all specified
                              continuations treat identically.
  Fiber                       The set of states that a re-encoding sends to the same code.
  GT                          Generalized transformer: a layer organized as routing, transport, lifting,
                              optional reconciliation and re-encoding.
  CD                          Compression-driven distinction architecture: executable structure,
                              residuals, refitting and structural search.
  RBII                        Resource-bounded incremental induction: bounded program discovery,
                              reuse, and online prediction and adaptation.
  MDL                         Minimum description length: prefer the explanation that minimizes the
                              total length of model plus data.
  Dependency tower            Nested families of observations of selected interactions, with explicit
                              order and resource budgets.
  STLM-style guide            A scorer, trained on search history, for logical or program templates,
                              their contexts and search actions.
  Proxy                       A cheap ranking of candidates from the current residual, with no learned
                              history.
