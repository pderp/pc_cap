How to use these notes

There is one section per slide. Each begins with a reminder of what is on the slide and a suggested
way of talking it through. Then every term and idea that appears on the slide is explained twice:
first in plain words, the way one might explain it to a first-year computing student, and then, after
the marker in symbols, in the notation of the report. Each section ends with questions the audience
is likely to ask, and short answers. You will not say most of this aloud. It is here so that whatever a
listener asks about a slide, the answer is on the page for that slide.
All numbers are taken from the report. Losses are in bits per byte (bpb) on held-out text, lower is
better, and unless stated otherwise they are means over five random seeds that share one frozen base
model, one corpus and one dictionary.

The argument in five sentences

A transformer layer does six separable jobs, and attention with an MLP is one way of doing them.
One of the six, reconciling conflicting evidence, is left empty in a standard transformer, and it is
the natural home of predictive-coding inference. Compression-driven distinction (CD) adds a slow
process that changes the structure of the network, beside PC’s fast inference and its slower weight
learning. A small system of this kind, attached as a “cap” to a frozen transformer, improves prediction
substantially; most of the gain comes from a richer memory, and smaller, consistent gains come from
structure search and from settling. As a learning rule PC ties with backpropagation so far, and the
questions that would decide whether PC becomes central to this program are ones this audience is
equipped to answer.

What fitting a fixed architecture leaves out

  On the slide. Five numbered rows: continual learning; learning during use; exchange of capabilities; neural–
  symbolic links; creativity off-distribution. Each has a one-line description.


What to say

“Today’s models are fitted in one phase and used in another, with the weights frozen in between. That
split is why they work so well, and it is also why five things we would like are awkward. None of
them is impossible for a transformer. The question is whether an architecture that is explicit about
what it preserves, how new modules are found, and how adaptation is kept local makes them easier
to combine and to measure. This talk has evidence on the first two and proposals for the rest.”
Do not linger. Read the five names, give one sentence to the first, and move on.

Terms and concepts

Fitting a fixed architecture. Choosing the numbers inside a network whose wiring has been decided
beforehand. Training changes the numbers and never the wiring.
in symbols. θ ∗ = arg minθ E(x,y) [− log pθ (y | x)] for a fixed function family {pθ }. In a CD system the
family itself, indexed by a structure M , is also searched.

Continual learning; stored versus reachable knowledge. Learning something new without losing
the old. A frozen model keeps its old skill only by learning nothing, so it does not count. A perfect
archive does not count either if the system can no longer find the right entry. The slide separates
having knowledge from reaching it, and slide 12 shows a failure of the second kind.
in symbols. For an old domain A and new domain B, with checkpoints θA before and θA→B after
adaptation,

      FA = LA (θA→B ) − LA (θA ) (forgetting),             GB = LB (θA ) − LB (θA→B ) (new learning).

Both must be reported, since a frozen model has FA = GB = 0.

Learning during use; timescales. Four things should be able to go on together: answering, briefly
adjusting internal state, updating weights, and discovering new structure. They run at different
speeds, like a shop that serves customers all day, restocks each evening and refits the premises once a
year, without closing.

M ← M ′ after a search episode. A versioned predictor is published atomically so that a running

Module; exchange of capabilities. A skill packaged so that it can be handed to another system,

narrower question here is whether one explicit architecture makes the five goals easier to combine
and to measure together.

Slide 3 about 30 seconds
The core claim

  On the slide. “A transformer layer does six jobs. Attention plus an MLP is one way of doing them. Name the
  jobs and the alternatives become design choices: lookups, small programs, explicit records, and an inference
  step that settles.”


What to say

Read the first two sentences and pause. Then: “Everything that follows comes from taking those six
jobs seriously as separate interfaces. Once they are separate, you can swap in a dictionary lookup for
attention, or keep records instead of averaging them, or, the part that concerns this room, put an
inference step where the standard transformer has none.”

Terms and concepts

relevance, turns the scores into weights that sum to one, and takes the weighted average of what
those positions offer.

Lookups, small programs, explicit records. Three alternatives to soft attention. A lookup finds the
exact matching entry in a table. A small program computes an answer by a short explicit procedure,
such as “find the most recent earlier occurrence of this phrase and return what followed it.” An
explicit record keeps an item whole, with a note of where it came from, where attention would blend
it into an average.
in symbols. A lookup is routing with a point mass: aij = 1 for the matching j and 0 elsewhere.
A program is a payload map V or feature map g given by executable code. A record is an atom
(j, V (xj )) of the tagged measure on slide 4, left unaveraged.

An inference step that settles. A short iterative computation, carried out before the layer commits
to an output, that adjusts temporary state until expectations and evidence agree as well as they can.
This is the map S. In a standard transformer it is the identity.

Questions you may get

Isn’t “six jobs” just a relabelling of any function? It has content because it imposes restrictions. All
access between positions must be declared through the routing weights and payloads, the local maps
see only that evidence, the order of observation is explicit, and the costs of reading, storing, inferring
and rewriting are counted separately.

One layer, six jobs

  On the slide. Six boxes in a row, joined by arrows: Need, Route, Transport, Lift, Reconcile (highlighted),
  Re-encode. Below them the layer equation. Below that: a standard block has jobs 1–3 as attention with an
  immediate average, job 4 as the MLP, job 6 as residual plus norm, and job 5 as the identity.


What to say

“Think of each position in the text as a small desk with some notes on it. In one layer, the desk first
decides what it needs. It picks which other desks to consult. It brings back what they have, keeping
a note of who said what. It works out some helpful derived facts. If its sources disagree, it takes a
moment to reconcile them. Then it writes up a fresh set of notes for the next layer.
In a standard transformer, the first three are attention, which averages what it fetched immediately,
the fourth is the MLP, and the last is the residual connection and layer norm. The fifth job, the
highlighted one, is skipped. It is the identity map. That empty slot is where predictive-coding
inference goes.”
Then point at the equation and read it from the inside out: “route and fetch, put it beside what I
already have, add features, settle, re-encode.”

Slide 5 about 1 minute
Averaging too early loses the binding

  On the slide. The sentence “The captain is Mira. The cook is Leo. The captain is           .” Two memory cards:
  A (captain–Mira, cook–Leo) and B (captain–Leo, cook–Mira). A third card: the uniform average of the records
  is the same for A and B. Bottom line: route selectively, or lift a joint feature before aggregating.


What to say

“Here is why the order of those jobs is not a technicality. Two ships have the same two jobs and
the same two people, with the roles swapped. Write each fact as ‘role plus name’ and average the
facts. The two ships now look identical. You know there is a captain and a cook, a Mira and a Leo,
and you have lost who is which. Nothing you compute afterwards can get it back, because the two
inputs have become the same input. If you look up only the captain’s record, or compute a joint
role-and-name feature before averaging, the information survives. I am not claiming transformers
fail at this. Several heads can solve it. I am showing that where information gets thrown away is a
design decision, and the framework makes you take it in the open.”

Terms and concepts

Binding. Knowing which things go together: that Mira goes with captain and Leo with cook. It is
distinct from knowing which things are present.

One-hot code. The simplest way to turn a category into numbers: a list of zeros with a single one
marking the category.
in symbols. Roles: captain = e1 , cook = e2 in R2 . Names: Mira = e1 , Leo = e2 in R2 . A record is
the concatenation [role; name] ∈ R4 .

Uniform average of the records.
in symbols. Memory A holds [e1 ; e1 ] = (1, 0, 1, 0) and [e2 ; e2 ] = (0, 1, 0, 1), whose average is 12 (1, 1, 1, 1).
Memory B holds [e1 ; e2 ] = (1, 0, 0, 1) and [e2 ; e1 ] = (0, 1, 1, 0), whose average is also 12 (1, 1, 1, 1). For
any function f , however nonlinear, f (avgA ) = f (avgB ).

“No later nonlinearity can bring it back”. A function gives the same output on the same input. Once
two situations have been mapped to one vector, no downstream cleverness can separate them. In the
language of slide 6, the average has merged two states that a later question (“who is the captain?”)
distinguishes.

Route selectively. Read only the captain’s record.
in symbols. Put all routing weight on the matching source: ai,captain = 1. The payload returned is
[e1 ; e1 ] under A and [e1 ; e2 ] under B, which differ.

Lift a joint feature before aggregating. Compute a “role and name together” feature for each
record first, then average.

Slide 6 about 1 minute
What a compressed state must keep

  On the slide. A formula for the behavioral distance dC . A statement: two states may share one code exactly
  when no declared later computation can tell them apart. Three cards: the price of merging (δ/2); precision
  from later use (the Fisher matrix G); CD as a GT layer (rules, exceptions, index, budgets).


What to say

“Job six was ‘keep what later computations need.’ Here is what that means exactly. Two situations
can be filed under the same note if every later question we care about gets the same answer from
either. If you file together two situations that some later question treats differently, that question
must be answered wrongly for at least one of them, and I can tell you by how much. For this audience
the middle card is the interesting one. If you ask how sensitive later predictions are to a small change
in the state, you get a Fisher matrix, which is a precision matrix over directions in state space, set
by downstream use. Nobody has yet put that into a PC energy. The last card is the definition of a
CD network: a layer whose final step tries to approximate this ideal, using reusable rules, a list of
exceptions to them, and an index, under a budget.”

Terms and concepts

State; code. The state is everything on the desk. The code is the shorter note written in its place.
in symbols. A re-encoding is a map r : Z → U from states to codes, with |U | typically much smaller

declaring only the uses that survive it.

Questions you may get

Who chooses C? The designer, and that is a real limitation. The guarantee covers only the declared
uses. In practice C is a finite set of probes, replay examples and protected behaviors.
Is this the information bottleneck? It is closely related. The bottleneck trades compression against
mutual information with one target variable. Here the target is a family of downstream computations,
and the worst-case distance gives the exact δ/2 statement.

Slide 7 about 1 minute
Three ways to respond to an error

  On the slide. Three cards: revise the answer (PC inference, fast, every token); revise the habit (PC local
  rule or BP, after outcomes); revise the machinery (CD structural search, slow, background). Bottom line: PC
  already separates the first two, CD adds the third, and all of it can sit in a cap on a frozen transformer.


What to say

“When a learner gets something wrong, it can change three different things. It can change its mind
about this case: maybe the copy reader deserves more trust on this particular sentence. It can change
a habit: maybe it should generally trust that reader in contexts like this one. Or it can change its
equipment: maybe it needs a reader it does not have. Predictive coding already gives you the first

What to say

“That is the aim: a learner that can preserve what it knows, reach it when needed, revise it, and share
it, all while running. The plan is staged, and each stage has a test it must pass before we spend more.
First, replicate on fresh data. Then a base model of tens of millions of parameters, where the cap has
to beat a strong ordinary adapter at equal memory and compute. Then a billion-parameter open
model with several caps working together. Only after that, exchange of modules between machines,
and a frontier-scale base. I would be glad to talk with anyone who wants to take on one of the
problems on the previous slide.”

Terms and concepts

Preserve, reach, revise, share. These four verbs correspond to the goals of slide 2: retention; access,
which slide 12 showed to be a separate matter from retention; the three kinds of revision of slide 7;
and exchange of modules.

Fresh small corpora; growing readers; retained gates; memory ceilings; task streams. The
immediate work. Use text that played no part in development. Let the reader memory grow during
use. Keep a copy of the old gate alongside the new one as one form of protection. Fix a total memory
budget so that “remember everything” is not an available answer. Test on long sequences of domains,
with returns to earlier ones.

A strong ordinary adapter at matched memory and compute. An adapter is a small trainable
add-on to a frozen model, such as LoRA. The cap has to beat one when both are given the same
memory and the same computation. If it does not, the extra machinery is not paying for itself.
in symbols. A budget identity keeps the comparison honest. With base cost CB per token, cap
fast-path cost CC , K candidate trials of cost CT every B tokens, and background work CH over T
tokens, the average cost per token is C = CB + CC + KCT /B + CH /T . If a search costs 2000 CB , the
fast path 0.02 CB and background work 0.01 CB , then an overhead ceiling of 10% allows one search
per 2000/0.07 ≈ 28,600 tokens. Structure search therefore has to be an occasional investment whose
results are reused.

Composable caps; symbolic readers; held-out compositions. Several caps active together (the first
card of slide 13). Readers whose distribution comes from a reasoner or a program, for instance a PLN
query or a MeTTa function. Tests in which the required combination of operations never appeared in
training (slide 2).

Module exchange between workers with different data. The decentralized goal. Each worker
discovers modules on its own data, publishes them, and tests what it receives before adopting it.
